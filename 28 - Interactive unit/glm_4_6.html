Introduction

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite, orbiting our planet at an average distance of 384,400 km. It takes about 27.3 days for the Moon to complete one orbit around Earth.

The Moon doesn't produce its own light - it shines because it reflects sunlight from its surface. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different portions of its illuminated side, creating what we call "moon phases."

When the Earth, Moon, and Sun line up in just the right way, we get special events called eclipses. During a solar eclipse, the Moon blocks the Sun's light from reaching Earth. During a lunar eclipse, Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon!

Summary

Moon as Earth's Satellite

The Moon orbits Earth at an average distance of 384,400 km, completing one orbit every 27.3 days.

Moon Phases

As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different portions of its illuminated side, creating phases from new moon to full moon.

Eclipses

Solar eclipses occur when the Moon blocks the Sun, while lunar eclipses happen when Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon.

Relative Sizes

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 109 times wider than Earth!
